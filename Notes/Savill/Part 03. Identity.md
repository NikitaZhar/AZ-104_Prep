## Introduction

Welcome to the identity module. It may seem odd that identity is covered so early in the course, but there are good reasons for that. In our cloud world, identity is the new security perimeter. You can no longer lock things away in your network with just a firewall — instead, identity is the place where you assign permissions to the various resources you need. As resources move to living in the cloud, you can't rely on the network as that single perimeter anymore, so it's critical to understand, architect, and operate your identity solution correctly. That also includes educating your users so they're aware of the different types of attacks they may experience.

This module looks at the basics of identity, including both traditional Active Directory Domain Services and Entra ID, which is the new name for what used to be Azure AD (if you hear "Entra ID," realize that's the new name for Azure AD). We'll also look at how Entra relates to your Azure subscriptions, and at conditional access — a huge feature of Entra that lets you place requirements on accessing resources, such as requiring strong authentication like multi-factor authentication or pass keys. We'll also look at key concepts such as just-in-time permissions through things like privileged identity management. The recurring theme is: think just enough and just in time.

## The need for identity

Why do we need identities at all? In our interactions with services and resources, there's often a requirement to give different identities different levels of access — not everyone gets the same access. The goal is always to give whatever identity we're dealing with the minimum possible set of permissions, just enough to perform the required function — i.e., just enough / least privilege.

> **Exam tip:** Having more permissions than required is a risk. It increases the risk of accidental damage (you have so many permissions that a mistake has a massive impact) or deliberate abuse (a bad actor gets hold of the credential and can do more damage). Always aim for the minimum possible permissions to do the job, and only add permissions at the time they're actually needed rather than leaving them sitting around all the time.

Sets of permissions or actions are typically grouped into roles, and a role is then granted to a specific security principal at a specific scope. Rather than granting hundreds of individual permissions, we group them into roles — sets of permissions or actions that are commonly required together to perform a certain function — and then grant a certain identity a certain role at a specific scope. The scope could be the entire identity system, or, thinking about Azure, it could be just a certain subscription or a certain resource. This is called a role assignment: granting a role to an identity.

When using the term "identity," it's important to realize there are different types. We tend to default to thinking of a human being, but that's not always the case — an identity could absolutely be a human, but it could also be an application, some type of automation (a script that has to perform functions against a resource, so it needs to act as an identity), or a device, among other things. The whole reason for scope is so that permissions exist only at the point they're actually required.

You might wonder why you need a separate identity for every app, every human, or every automation — why not just share them? There are multiple challenges with that. Different people and different automations need to perform different functions and need different sets of permissions, so if they share an identity, the permissions that identity has become the sum of everything required to do all the different things it has to do — which means it's over-permissioned for any one particular requirement.

> **Exam tip:** Sharing an identity also destroys auditability. If multiple actors — multiple humans, multiple automations — are using the same account, you have no way to tell which one actually did a given thing; you just see a log entry for the shared identity. You need that granularity: roles are granted to specific principals at specific scopes, actors should be uniquely identifiable, and you need a central store for identities plus the ability to use them.

So the whole point is: you want to have identities, you want to be able to grant permissions to certain types of resources, and if you want to use a certain identity, you need to be able to prove you're allowed to act as that identity. There has to be a central store of identities that holds the identities and holds the ability for someone to prove they're allowed to use one — this is commonly known as an identity provider (IdP).

This central store, the identity provider, maintains the list of security principals — the accounts you can authenticate against — and at a minimum is responsible for enabling that authentication so someone can act as their identity. Authentication (auth) means proving you are who you say you are, or at least proving you have the knowledge that says you're allowed to use this identity. That could be a password, possession of a certificate you can use for a cryptographic operation, or possession of a physical token. You perform an authentication against the identity provider, and it gives you some kind of token.

Various applications trust particular identity providers to perform this authentication. Because they trust the identity provider, this is where you can grant roles — authorize the identity to do certain things. If Bob has an account, you can give Bob a specific role for a particular application; after authenticating to the identity provider and getting a token for that application, Bob can give that token to the application and consume it.

From an Entra perspective, you have a certain tenant, and if you're using Active Directory or M365, you're already using Entra, as we'll see. Entra ID is Microsoft's enterprise identity provider. It might also perform certain types of authorization — for example, requiring strong authentication like MFA. The key element in this picture is that the identity provider sits in the middle: there are the humans/users, there are the applications, and the identity provider is in the center. It holds the accounts and owns the accounts — and there are some caveats with that, which have led to a new type of approach.

## Decentralized identity

Decentralized identity is really more of a "be aware of" topic, but it is gaining momentum (there's a full 90-minute video on this if you're really interested).

First, ask yourself: as a human being, what really is your identity? It's not really the things you say about yourself — if you just tell someone something, they probably don't trust you on your word alone. You can't get on a plane at an airport, or get a loan from a bank, based purely on your word. You need a government-issued ID, proof of employment from your company — artifacts from other entities that others trust, which you can then present and have verified. A government ID is tamper-proof, has a hologram, so the verifier can confirm it really came from the proper authority, and you typically store these artifacts in a physical wallet. The digital world is no different: your digital identity is really about what other trusted entities will attest about you — it's the sum of what all those other people will attest.

With decentralized identity, remember that in the traditional picture the identity provider is in the center: the user authenticates to the IdP that holds the credential, and the app trusts the identity provider. We're trusting that the identity provider won't overshare the information it knows, that it does a good job confirming identities, and that it's transparent about what it shares. If you really think about it, you don't actually own that identity — it could be taken from you at any time if there's a disagreement with the identity provider or you don't adhere to its policies.

With decentralized identity, the user is in the middle of the picture. They own and control the use of the identity and the information shared that makes up that identity — the user has complete control over what actually gets shared or doesn't. Other entities issue verifiable credentials where your identifier is the subject, and you can choose to share these with a verifier, and choose exactly what is shared — you don't have to share all the information. It's rooted in a trust system.

The mechanics: the user has their own digital wallet — some kind of user agent/application — and one or more decentralized identifiers (DIDs). A DID can be resolved to a DID document that provides information and the cryptographic material required to validate it. Remember, it's about what others say and attest about you, not what you say about yourself. So there's an issuer — a government agency, an employer, a store, whatever — which has its own DID. The issuer creates a verifiable credential where your DID is the subject, populates claims about you into it, and gives it to you; it goes into your digital wallet. Underneath all of this is some kind of trust system — it varies, and could be based on something like a blockchain — but it's rooted in this shared trust system.

Now consider a verifier: it says it wants some information, and you have to give consent — you're willing to share, with full control over it — and you can then share that verifiable credential with the verifier, who can validate it through the trust system via cryptographic operations. A key point: you don't have to share the entire verifiable credential. Maybe you have to prove you're over 21 without sharing your actual birth date — you can share just the fact that you're over 21. You have complete control, and the verifier can still validate the authenticity of the claims through the underlying trust system. Everything is bound in your wallet — you're not relying on a centralized identity provider to function. You are in the center of the picture, which is why it's called decentralized identity.

There are many open standards and wallet technologies around this — the Microsoft Authenticator app is one such wallet, in the form of Microsoft Entra Verified ID, but there are many others. Again, this is a side item worth being aware of — it's gaining traction, and you may occasionally see a company asking for a verified ID during onboarding — but it's not something you'll need to deal with heavily today. It's good to at least understand the concept. Now, onto the real focus: Entra ID.

## Enter Entra ID

Today, for most use cases — and certainly for Azure — you need a centralized identity provider. It hosts identities for your users, computers, applications, and groups, and is trusted by all your various subscriptions. The IdP for the Microsoft corporate world is Entra ID.

Entra ID was formerly known as Azure AD. It was renamed, which was a good decision — the "Azure AD" name was misleading, making you think it was traditional on-prem Active Directory running in the cloud, which it was not at all; it's a completely different type of directory service. "Entra" was a brand Microsoft started using for a range of identity and security-related technologies (think of it as "the entrance" to your services), and the new name better represents what it really is.

All Microsoft clouds require you to have an Entra tenant, which holds the accounts and policies specific to your organization. Azure, Microsoft 365, Dynamics 365, etc. all require you to have an Entra tenant, and the Microsoft services you've purchased as an organization will trust your specific Entra tenant. Your company has its own Entra tenant, containing your users, groups, devices, and the specific services you use — Azure subscriptions, M365, third-party SaaS applications — which all trust your specific tenant. That's why, if you're in your company's Azure subscription, you'll see your company's users as available to grant permissions to; if you're in your company's SharePoint site, likewise — you don't see other companies' users.

> **Exam tip:** Entra has thousands of existing federations with major third-party SaaS applications, and you can add your own — additional OAuth and OIDC apps, and you can even publish your on-prem apps via Entra, so it's not just for Microsoft properties. The goal is that a single identity — whether user or automation — can be used against all the different applications in your environment. Your Azure subscription trusts one specific tenant, not just any tenant in the world.

As already mentioned, Entra ID is not "Active Directory in the cloud" — they are completely different. Yes, both provide identity in some way, but they do it in a completely different manner. Traditional Active Directory Domain Services was focused on providing identity capabilities for a closed network and a specific set of devices. It primarily used Kerberos for authentication (also NTLM, though that's being deprecated), and LDAP (Lightweight Directory Access Protocol) for queries. These protocols work over a wide range of ports between entities on the network, and they rely on a shared secret with Active Directory Domain Services as part of the authentication process — machines actually have to join the domain. There's a hierarchical structure via organizational units (OUs), to which you can apply different policies and delegate management (e.g., give someone permission to manage objects just within an OU and its child OUs).

There is no structure like that in Entra. Today's services typically live in the cloud and communicate over the network — you can't and wouldn't want to open all the ports required for something like Kerberos authentication, and Kerberos requires a shared secret that most cloud-based devices simply won't have. Instead of authenticating directly as you, you often want to consent for some service to work on your behalf. You may be thinking you could do this before with Active Directory Federation Services (ADFS) or third-party solutions — and that's true: you could manually create federations with SaaS applications, but you'd need a whole set of infrastructure to manage — all the certificates, a bunch of public-facing components to interface with and protect. Most organizations don't want to deal with that anymore; it's easier to let Entra do it with its built-in federations.

Compare this to Entra: it's built for the cloud and designed to use cloud protocols — TLS encryption over HTTP(S), so effectively a single port. It speaks cloud authentication: OpenID Connect (OIDC) for authentication, OAuth 2 for authorization, and it can also do SAML and WS-Fed. It has some limited Kerberos capability built for specific Azure services (some Azure Files functionality, Azure Virtual Desktop), but that's very limited. For management purposes or querying objects, it uses a RESTful API — again over an encrypted port — and there's the Microsoft Graph, a standard for talking not just to Entra but to information about your M365 environment, Dynamics environment, and so on — a very rich set of CRUD capabilities. There is no concept of OUs in Entra — it's flat. You'll hear about administrative units later on for more granular delegation, but that's not a hierarchy of objects; it's really just a way to restrict certain types of administrative permission.

There are different licensing SKUs available: Free, and then Entra ID P1 and P2, licensed per user per month. There's also something called the Entra Suite, which builds on Entra ID P1 — you have to have P1 licensed, and then you add the Entra Suite on top, which includes all the P2 features plus a whole bunch of rich capabilities like private access and Verified ID (part of the decentralized identity capability mentioned earlier). It also has the full set of rich governance features — P2 has some of these but not all of them.

> **Exam tip:** P2 adds identity protection — understanding risk, both user risk and sign-in risk. Free has some basic capabilities, but for most organizations you're going to want at minimum the P1 license. Because these licenses are per user per month, you don't have to license every user the same way — some users with more basic requirements can have P1, others (e.g., admins doing more risk-based work) can have P2, others might need the Entra Suite. You give licenses very specifically based on the features each person needs, rather than giving everyone the highest common denominator.

## How do you get Entra ID?

The good news is you probably already have it, since Entra ID is the identity provider used by Microsoft services, and huge numbers of other services — including third-party SaaS solutions — now tie into Entra as the standard identity provider for everything they want to do.

Your organization has its own Entra tenant with some name; by default the tenant name is a `*.onmicrosoft.com` name, but you can change that. The tenant holds your users, groups, devices, and policies — you may also hear it called a "directory," meaning the same thing. The tenant is named whatever you specified at creation time (e.g., `savtech.onmicrosoft.com`).

If you look at an Azure subscription, it trusts a specific directory — you can see which tenant it trusts, and there's an option to change directory (requiring strong authentication to do so).

> **Exam tip:** Changing the trusted directory on a subscription looks like an easy button to push, but it would have a huge impact. That directory contains your objects, applications, and groups — the things you grant permissions to within your Azure subscription. If you tell it to trust a different tenant with different identities, all of your configured role assignments would be lost, and things like managed identities would not be there — you'd lose all of your permissioning. So while you technically can change the tenant, if you have actual services deployed, you generally don't want to — it will break things.

By default the tenant has a `.onmicrosoft.com` name, but you can add custom domain names, which is what you're going to do in practice — you're not going to want your users to see something `.onmicrosoft.com`. In the Entra portal, under your identity area's settings, you'll find domain names, where you can add additional domain names by proving ownership (typically by creating a DNS record in your zone). You can add a custom domain (e.g., `saveltech.net`) alongside the default `.onmicrosoft.com` one, and set the custom one as your primary DNS name so it's used going forward.

If you're an M365 customer — which many people are — you already have an Entra tenant. There is no such thing as a separate "M365 directory service" — it's using Entra. In the M365 admin center you can see users, but that's still just hooking into the Microsoft Graph, still hooking into your Entra tenant — it's not a separate tenant in any way. The users you see there are exactly the same users you see in the Entra portal, so don't think "I don't use Entra, I use M365" — it's an Entra tenant; it was just created as part of your onboarding to the service, and you're leveraging it via that.

There used to be a separate Entra PowerShell module for automations, but now you just use the [[Glossary#Microsoft Graph PowerShell module|Microsoft Graph PowerShell module]] — there aren't separate modules you need anymore. You're managing Entra primarily through the Entra portal, though there are some elements in the Azure portal and the Office portal as well, and via the Microsoft Graph through APIs and PowerShell.

You can create additional tenants as needed, and they're free. In the Entra portal, under Overview, there's a "Managed tenants" option from which you can add/create tenants — either Workforce (for your company) or External (customer-facing, with customer accounts). You may end up with multiple tenants for different purposes (labs, experimentation, etc.).

> **Exam tip:** Ideally, you don't want to just create huge numbers of tenants — it complicates things. There may be legitimate scenarios (a sandbox tenant, or a tenant inherited from a company merger), but you want to keep things as simple as possible where you can. Note also that licensing purchased in one tenant can, under certain conditions, apply to other tenants your organization owns.

## Entra ID Objects

There are many types of objects in Entra. Most of them are security principals — some entity you can be authenticated as, which has some way to prove you're allowed to use it: a secret like a password, or a public key where you need the private key to perform a cryptographic operation.

**Users** are the most fundamental type of identity. The key point: when we say "user," it should be some carbon-based life form, tied to a specific person.

> **Exam tip:** Don't share user accounts between multiple people — you lose the ability to audit, and accounts tend to accumulate inflated permissions over time. The emphasis on the carbon-based nature is important: if you have an automation or application running that isn't sitting at a keyboard, doesn't use a mouse, and can't operate a phone or use a token, it should not have a user account — there are better options (service principals, covered below). One reason: specific requirements often get applied to human-based accounts, such as mandatory MFA, and an automation can't comply with a multi-factor authentication prompt. This is actually one of the problems people are facing right now — automations that were given regular user accounts are now breaking as Microsoft rolls out mandatory MFA, forcing a move to the correct approach.

In the Entra portal, the Users list shows attributes for each account, and you can apply filters to restrict what you're seeing. Basic properties include display name, user principal name (which contains your primary custom DNS name), and user type — member or guest. Member is considered someone within your organization, while guest is an external identity, and there are differences in basic rights between members and guests (for example, you can limit what guests can see when browsing the directory, compared to members).

There's also an "on-premises sync" column showing yes/no — very often you'll have Active Directory Domain Services and be synchronizing objects from there — and an "identities" column showing where the identity actually originates: most will be your own tenant (the `.onmicrosoft.com` name), but some may come from an external Azure AD tenant, Facebook, a phone-based identity, Google, or a Microsoft account. It's not a direct correlation that being an external identity means you have to be a guest — that person might be within your organization but just in a different tenant, so you might not want them marked as a guest.

It's easy to create new users one at a time, or in bulk via a CSV file that populates most of the details, or by hooking into HR systems — via a provisioning agent, when a user is added in the HR system it calls the Entra provisioning agent, which calls Active Directory and creates the user (or creates it directly in Entra if there's no Active Directory). Looking at a user's details, you can see a huge amount of information: office location, job title, on-premises detail replicated from on-prem (e.g., which OU it's in), audit logs of changes to the account, sign-in log information, group memberships, application assignments, licenses, Azure role assignments, and the specific authentication methods configured for them.

**Groups** are commonly used — specifically security groups. If you want to give access to resources, applications, or grant licenses, it's better to use a group than to give access directly to a particular user.

> **Exam tip:** If you grant permission directly to a user, it's easy to forget you did that — as they change roles, they accumulate an ever-creeping set of permissions. If you grant the permission to a group instead, and users are added to or removed from that group, their access simply follows the group's membership.

Group membership doesn't have to be purely manual — you can create a dynamic group, where you specify rules based on user or device attributes, and as those attributes change, membership updates automatically (e.g., based on department name, or hire date). For example, in the portal, group membership type can be assigned or dynamic. A dynamic group might have a rule where job title must start with "Hero" or "Heroine" for automatic membership — if someone's job title changes to something else, they automatically fall out of the group. Another dynamic group example uses the employee hire date attribute (which might be populated by an HR system), with a rule such as "greater than or equal to the current time minus 365 days," i.e., anyone hired within the last year — useful for automatically granting new-joiner resources and losing that access once the new-joiner window passes.

As mentioned, an automation or application should not use a regular user account — instead it should leverage a **service principal**. In your tenant this is an app registration, which then has an associated secret, an associated certificate, or a federated credential. A federated credential lets an automation living elsewhere — say, in GitHub, or in another tenant — exchange a token from its own tenant for a token from yours, without having to maintain a secret in your tenant. For example, a GitHub Actions workflow doing a deployment doesn't need a stored secret; instead it's given a token from the GitHub identity provider, and you create a federation in your tenant saying that this particular GitHub identity is allowed to swap its token for a token from you — that identity can then be given permissions in your Azure subscriptions.

In app registrations, under certificates and secrets, you can add a secret, add a certificate, or add a federated credential — for example, a GitHub Actions workflow tied to a specific repo, org, and environment, which has no client secret at all but instead a federated credential configured. If you're not doing that, you can create a certificate (preferred over a secret), and your automations/applications can authenticate using that. You can also create app registrations via PowerShell, which creates an Enterprise app — the service principal specific to your tenant.

> **Exam tip:** The whole point of using a service principal instead of a user account is that you don't want things like mandatory MFA enforced on an automation — service principals give you that flexibility.

There's also a **managed identity**, still a service principal, but a special type. It's used for Azure resources — imagine one Azure resource wants to interact with another, e.g., an automation running in an Azure Function that needs to talk to a Storage account or an Azure SQL database. Rather than having your process store a secret or certificate, a managed identity is automatically created and tied either to a specific resource or to a group of resources (user-assigned). Running inside that resource, you can simply request a token, and it's granted because the Azure control plane knows you're running within that particular resource. You get the token and use it against other resources where it's been given permissions.

> **Exam tip:** If you're running an Azure resource that needs to talk to another Azure resource, you generally want to use a managed identity — no secret or certificate to store at all.

**Devices** have three different device states in Entra:

- **Registered** — usually about Windows client devices (Windows 10+), iOS, Android, macOS. The device is known to your tenant (so you can apply things like Mobile Device Management via Intune), but you're still signing in with a non-Entra account, perhaps a Microsoft account — good for a bring-your-own-device scenario.
- **Joined** — a step above registered; good for a corporate-owned device. You're actually signing in with Entra accounts.
- **Hybrid** (hybrid-joined) — the device is actually joined to your on-prem Active Directory Domain Services environment, so it's primarily managed through group policy, but it also gets registered, so it's known as part of Entra too — giving you a combination of capabilities.

Most organizations have Active Directory Domain Services, so users and groups are typically not created directly in Entra — instead they're maintained through Active Directory and replicate into Entra.

> **Exam tip:** Entra is not going to replace Active Directory for most companies. There's a certain trend toward a "modern desktop" approach, where client devices join Entra instead of Active Directory (using Intune for management) — great especially for remote/mobile users — but you're probably not getting rid of Active Directory, especially for servers.

When this replication relationship exists, it's critical to understand: **Active Directory is the source of truth**. The flow runs from Active Directory Domain Services into Entra — objects, users, groups flow that direction. There is some limited write-back capability, including some group write-back (you can create a group in Entra and, if users replicate from AD, replicate that group back into your Active Directory) — but the primary flow runs from AD to Entra.

Even though Active Directory is the source of truth, there are huge numbers of HR integrations. Whatever your HR system is, there's a provisioning service for Entra — when a new employee joins, your HR system talks to the Entra provisioning service; if you have Active Directory, that provisioning service talks to a provisioning agent that creates the account in Active Directory, and the user then replicates to Entra; if you don't have Active Directory, it's created directly in Entra. This avoids separate manual operations for joiner/mover/leaver scenarios — you can automate all of that through provisioning services (available for specific HR systems, plus a provisioning API so you can hook in from virtually anything).

## AD to Entra ID Sync

As already noted, AD is the source of truth: objects — users, groups — flow primarily from AD to Entra, with some limited write-back possible. There are very specific rules around the relationship between Active Directory Domain Services, the technology used for synchronization, and the Entra tenant.

> **Exam tip:** An Entra tenant can only sync from one Entra Connect Sync instance (you can have a second instance in staging mode for disaster recovery scenarios, but only one is actively syncing). That Entra Connect instance can replicate from multiple domains and even multiple forests, but it will only talk to one tenant.

One Active Directory can sync to multiple Entra tenants — for example, if you're using both a commercial cloud and GovCloud and want to populate user accounts to both — by having multiple Connect Sync instances. However, only one of those can perform write-back; you cannot write back from more than one, because that would cause confusion. Microsoft's documentation on supported topologies walks through these legal configurations: one connector per tenant (though a connector can merge multiple forests), a full mesh topology, a staging server, and the scenario of one AD talking to multiple tenants where only one is allowed to write back.

**Entra Connect Sync** was the original sync solution — you run an instance of it on a Windows OS server. It maintains the idea of connector spaces (one for Active Directory, one for Entra) and merges objects together in something called a metaverse. This means you're running and maintaining an engine on a server instance — including upgrades.

The alternative is **Entra Cloud Sync**, which runs a very thin provisioning agent on a couple of servers — it could be your domain controllers.

> **Exam tip:** Since domain controllers are tier-zero servers, the provisioning agent used by Entra Cloud Sync needs the same tier of security posture. This is the same provisioning agent used for HR system integrations — the one that creates objects in AD when HR tells the Entra service about a new hire.

There are functional differences between the two, and the overall direction is toward Cloud Sync — for example, Cloud Sync can synchronize from multiple disconnected AD forests (forests that can't talk to each other), which Connect Sync cannot do; but there are still a few things Connect Sync can do today that Cloud Sync can't, such as pass-through authentication. They can coexist, just not sync the same objects — you could block certain OUs from Connect Sync and sync just those with Cloud Sync as part of a migration. It's common today to see environments that have retired Connect Sync entirely and use only Cloud Sync, running through provisioning agents installed on domain controllers, with configurations mapping the replication of properties between Entra ID and AD in both directions.

## Authentication & Authorization

There's a huge difference between authentication and authorization — the terms often get mixed up, but they do very different things.

> **Exam tip:** Authentication is all about proving you are who you say you are, or proving you're allowed to use a certain identity — that proof could be something you know (a password), something you have (a phone — receiving a text, a call, or a code from an app on it), or something you are (a biometric — fingerprint, retinal scan, facial scan). Authorization is about what that identity can do on a resource or service — certain roles, a certain set of access. These are very different concepts.

## Authentication Options

If you create an account directly in Entra, its password is stored in Entra, and you always authenticate against the cloud. But once you introduce an external identity system like Active Directory, you get additional choices: password hash sync (authenticate against the cloud), pass-through authentication (a hybrid-type solution), and federation.

In Active Directory, you don't store the raw password of a user — you store the password hash. The recommended first step is **password hash sync**: it takes that password hash, runs it through its own hashing process, adds a per-user salt, and runs it through a thousand hashing iterations — effectively meaning it cannot be reversed — resulting in a "hash of the hash," which is what gets replicated to Entra. Now the user account in Entra has this hash-of-the-hash stored, so the user can authenticate directly against Entra without needing to talk to Active Directory at all — it's pure cloud authentication, with no reliance on your on-prem directory or connectivity to it.

> **Exam tip:** Even if you opt to use a different authentication method, you should still enable password hash sync for a couple of reasons: Entra has capabilities that scour the dark web for leaked credentials using this hash, and can flag a user risk if a password has leaked; and if you're relying on some other method involving an external system (federation, pass-through) and your network or that system breaks, password hash sync can serve as a "break the glass" fallback authentication method. This is generally the preferred approach.

Another approach is **pass-through authentication**. You're still authenticating against Entra, but the attempt adds an entry to a service bus queue that an agent on your domain controller pulls from, performs the authentication, and reports success or failure back — if good, the user is then authenticated. This is useful if you have specific requirements Entra doesn't natively support, such as time-window-based authentication or the ability to instantly block a user if something happens on your on-prem system. It's a hybrid authentication option, still reliant on your on-prem infrastructure.

The other option is **federation**. You have a completely separate identity provider (which could even be Active Directory itself), and you create a federated relationship between it and Entra. When you try to authenticate, you're redirected to authenticate against that external provider, which generates a token; you give that token to Entra, which then creates its own Entra token that you use against resources trusting Entra. Conditional access policies, subsequent auth, and refreshes all still go against Entra.

> **Exam tip:** You might use federation if you had some advanced authentication requirement not supported by Entra — but the list of things Entra doesn't support natively is pretty tiny today. Most companies are moving away from federation for their own authentication; it's far more common just to use password hash sync (Entra's own federation capabilities to third-party applications are a different, very common thing). The generally recommended order of preference is: password hash sync first; pass-through authentication only for a genuinely specific need; avoid federation for authenticating your own users if you can.

All of these methods can give a nice, seamless experience — a user in Active Directory accessing a resource that trusts Entra gets single sign-on or seamless sign-on without repeated authentication prompts. There's official documentation with a decision tree covering the pros and cons if you're unsure which to choose, but for most companies, password hash sync is the way to go.

## Roles and Administrative Units

The roles discussed here relate to Entra and to Microsoft SaaS solutions like M365 and Dynamics 365 — they are separate from Azure Resource Manager (ARM) roles and are treated very differently; ARM roles are covered later, under Azure governance and permissions.

There are a whole bunch of built-in roles in Entra — around AI/M365 Copilot, Entra itself, Exchange, Dynamics 365, and more. These roles simply have different sets of permissions.

> **Exam tip:** A role you always want to be careful of is Global Administrator — the all-powerful account. It's flagged as "privileged," meaning it contains permissions that could be misused, including elevation. Be very careful who you give that type of role to. Looking at any built-in role's description shows the exact permissions it's made up of.

By default, Entra roles can only be granted to users directly. If you want to grant a role to a group, that group has to be a very specific type — a **cloud group, role-assignable**. When creating a group, if you enable "Entra roles can be assigned to the group," the membership type becomes locked to Assigned — it can no longer be a dynamic group; membership must be added manually rather than replicated from Active Directory.

If the built-in roles aren't sufficient — and remember, we always want the minimum possible set of permissions — you can create your own custom role, either from scratch or by cloning an existing role and modifying it.

> **Exam tip:** There is a hard limit of up to 100 custom roles per tenant.

The goal is always least privilege: minimum possible permissions, at the minimum possible scope. Normally, when you assign a role, it applies to the entire tenant — every object that falls within that permission, tenant-wide. But you may not want that: imagine a branch office where you want the office administrator to be able to reset passwords just for users in that office, without giving them a full tenant-wide Helpdesk Administrator capability. Instead of scoping the assignment to the whole tenant, you can assign it to an **administrative unit** — a container you can put users, groups, and devices into, so the role only applies to objects within that unit.

Not all roles are available for this kind of scoping. Membership of an administrative unit can be dynamic or assigned, similar to groups, though with some limitations.

> **Exam tip:** If you add a group to an administrative unit, this does **not** automatically include the users who are members of that group. This is deliberate — otherwise someone could add a user into that group and effectively gain management over that user. You have to explicitly add the users themselves to the administrative unit to be able to manage them.

An example administrative unit might use a dynamic rule such as "user city equals Gotham," so users matching that property automatically get added, and then you could grant a role — such as Helpdesk Administrator — scoped only to that administrative unit, so the assigned admin can reset passwords for users in Gotham but nowhere else.

> **Exam tip:** To create an administrative unit you must be a Global Administrator or a Privileged Role Administrator, and you need a P1 license. (If you're just doing administrative work within an already-created administrative unit, you don't need a P1 license yourself.) Administrative units cannot be nested, and they cannot be used to grant permission to resources — you still use regular groups for that.

There's also a **restricted management administrative unit** — a more locked-down variant for scenarios like CEOs, other very important people, or important devices, where only specifically designated people should be able to manage those objects — not the regular tenant-wide admins.

> **Exam tip:** By default, even Global Administrators cannot manage objects inside a restricted management administrative unit. (Technically, since a Global Admin is essentially a super-user, they could modify the administrative unit to add themselves a role over it — but this would leave a clear audit log entry showing they did so.) This is most useful for keeping regular helpdesk admins away from super-important objects; there's full logging of everything happening across these units.

Most of the time, administrative units are used for scenarios like a manager needing some capability over the people who work for them, or a helpdesk scenario for a remote office. There's also a simplified site, `mystaff.microsoft.com`, offering a stripped-down interface where a delegated admin only sees the administrative units they have permission on — useful for giving to local helpdesk admins etc. who don't need (or shouldn't have) the full Entra admin experience.

## Privileged Identity Management

Privileged Identity Management (PIM) is about just-in-time access: only having the minimum rights you need, and only getting them when you actually need them. PIM lets you elevate up to an Entra role or an Azure Resource Manager role for a finite amount of time. You can require strong authentication, hook into conditional access policies via an authentication context, and it's integrated into the Azure portal's Identity and access management experience. You can also elevate to group membership or to owning a group. You can require a justification for elevating, require manager approval, require an associated ticket, and link the elevation to an authentication context (e.g., requiring a compliant device, a workstation, or phishing-resistant authentication via a passkey).

> **Exam tip:** With PIM, you must preassign eligibility to a user in advance — it's not ad hoc. You decide in advance that this user is allowed to elevate to a particular role (in Entra or in Azure) or to be a member of a particular group; the user then elevates on demand, or can schedule elevation for a future time. This is an Entra ID P2 feature.

There are different flavors of PIM based on whether you're managing Entra roles or Azure roles. Under settings, you can configure, per role, the maximum duration a user is allowed to activate for, whether particular MFA is required, or whether an authentication context must be met. An authentication context is essentially just a label (with an ID) that you can target from a conditional access policy, specifying requirements like a certain authentication strength or a compliant device — typically used for sensitive data scenarios, but it can also be linked to a PIM elevation to demand stronger authentication than "just MFA."

You can fully configure activation duration, whether justification/ticket/approval is required, and whether the assignment is permanent or time-bound. Assignments can be granted to users or to the special cloud role-assignable group type; you choose whether the assignment is "eligible" (must elevate) or "active" (already granted, no elevation needed), and whether it's permanently eligible or eligible only for a finite period. For Azure resources, you first pick the scope — a management group, subscription, or resource group — then configure the same kinds of settings. This is exactly how the Azure portal's own Identity and access management experience works too — adding a role assignment there offers an "assignment type" option, letting you set it as Eligible instead of Active, with a duration and PIM policy linkage.

From the end-user side, activating a role from the PIM experience shows what you're eligible for; you activate, may be prompted for strong authentication, can optionally set a future activation start time, and must provide a reason.

> **Exam tip:** The whole point of PIM is that you only hold elevated roles when you actually need them — they're not just sitting there granted all the time, which is a much better security posture.

## Entra Permissions Management

This is more of an on-demand, ad hoc capability, whereas PIM requires configuring eligibility in advance. With Entra Permissions Management, you don't need to have been pre-assigned permissions — you request the specific permission you need, at a very granular level, not just whole roles. It works across clouds — Azure, AWS, GCP (each cloud needs onboarding to the solution).

> **Exam tip:** A major capability here is right-sizing of permissions: it looks at the roles identities have and the permissions they actually use, and can flag, for example, "you haven't used this permission in 90 days, we recommend removing it," or recommend a custom role with a more restricted set of permissions based on actual usage — helping you achieve that minimum-possible-permissions goal.

This originated from an acquisition (CloudKnox) and is a separate license — a more advanced capability, but good to know it exists.

## Access Reviews

Historically, permissions creep constantly over time: as people change roles they get added to more and more things, but very rarely removed. **Access reviews** address this directly: you can perform periodic reviews of group memberships, app assignments, role assignments (both Entra and Azure Resource Manager), and access package assignments. Reviews can be performed by administrators, specific designated people, or even as a self-review, where you ask people to confirm they still need the permissions they have.

In the portal, under Identity governance, you can create a new access review targeting teams and groups, or applications. Via PIM, you can create access reviews for Entra roles; for Azure resources, you can pick a subscription and create a review for the permissions granted to Azure resources. You can make a review recurring, so it has to be redone periodically, or a one-off.

> **Exam tip:** Licensing for access reviews is split — some functionality is part of P2, and some is part of an enhanced governance license included in the Entra Suite. You need to check the specific type of access review you want to run to know the exact license required.

## MFA and Strong Authentication

The conversation is really moving beyond plain MFA toward the concept of strong authentication, because these mean different things and have very different susceptibility to being tricked.

It's well understood that passwords alone are bad. Entra offers some basic protections around passwords: smart lockout tries to protect a genuine user from being locked out by bad actors spamming the account with wrong passwords — it has an escalating lockout period and factors in the different locations attempts are coming from to help distinguish a good actor from a bad one. You can also add protection against easily guessed passwords, including your own custom banned password list and detection of variants (e.g., swapping an "o" for a zero); this protection can even be applied against Active Directory via a small on-prem agent.

> **Exam tip:** Even with these basic protections, plain MFA blocks around 99.2% of most types of attacks (per the latest figures at the time).

MFA means proving your identity with two or more of: something you know (password, PIN, a certain gesture), something you are (biometric), or something you have (phone, token). By definition, MFA requires two or more of these categories.

There are different levels of strength:

- A password alone is the weakest option — you don't want a password on its own at all.
- Password plus SMS or a phone call is better than nothing, but not fantastic — there are attack types like SIM cloning, which, while not the average attacker's tool, can be a concern if you're specifically targeted.
- Password plus an authenticator app or a time-based one-time passcode (physical token or an app such as Microsoft Authenticator) is significantly better.
- Passwordless options — Windows Hello for Business (uses the device's TPM plus a PIN or biometric gesture to complete authentication), certificate-based authentication, and FIDO2 pass keys.

> **Exam tip:** Even without a password, passwordless methods still count as strong authentication/MFA: Hello for Business is "something you have" (the device) plus "something you know" (PIN) or "something you are" (biometric gesture). A pass key might be on your phone, or a USB FIDO2 key — again unlocked via biometric or PIN — and a pass key is even stronger because it requires proximity to the device performing the authentication.

The bottom three of these methods (Hello for Business, certificate-based authentication, and pass keys) are **phishing-resistant**.

> **Exam tip:** Phishing-resistant means a remote bad actor cannot trick you into authenticating on their behalf, because authentication is either performed on the device itself or requires physical proximity to it — there's no way that proximity requirement can be faked remotely. Be careful with regular MFA prompts: if you push MFA at users constantly, they get MFA fatigue. The recommendation is to trigger MFA based on detected risk rather than constantly. With pass keys or Hello for Business, it's not a negative user experience, so you can just always require strong authentication by default — another reason pass keys are being pushed. (FIDO2 in some ways is another term for pass key, but with the added proximity requirement.)

The reason a number-matching authenticator-app prompt isn't considered phishing-resistant: a user has to type in a specific number shown to them, along with context about what app is requesting access and where the request is coming from, so they can't just muscle-memory click "yes." But a bad actor can still socially engineer this — pretending to be from the help desk, saying "we detected unusual activity, we're sending a test, tell us the number" — and trick the user into approving. Whereas the phishing-resistant methods all require actual physical proximity between the device performing the strong authentication and the device requesting it, so a remote attacker hundreds of miles away simply cannot be tricked into completing it.

Entra has the concept of **authentication strength**, which you can select in conditional access policies instead of just requiring MFA.

> **Exam tip:** Built-in authentication strengths include: regular MFA (a broad list of accepted methods), passwordless MFA (Hello for Business, pass keys, certificate-based authentication, and Microsoft Authenticator), and phishing-resistant MFA (notably, this one does **not** include the Authenticator app — that's the key distinguishing point). You can also create custom authentication strengths. In a conditional access grant control, you can require a specific authentication strength instead of just "require MFA" — useful for sensitive apps where you don't want to trust something like an SMS text.

## Entra MFA Features

With Entra ID P1, you get access to all of these different MFA options. Without P1, you can use security defaults, which give some basic capabilities (a Global Admin gets additional options too).

> **Exam tip:** Starting in 2024, Microsoft began rolling out mandatory MFA, expanding over time from the Azure portal and Entra admin center to PowerShell, the CLI, and infrastructure-as-code tools. This directly connects to the earlier point about service principals: if you used a regular user account for your automation instead of a service principal, and mandatory MFA now applies to that account, you're stuck — there's no way for an automation to complete an MFA prompt. This is exactly why automations need to use service principals, so they don't get caught by human-oriented MFA requirements. As noted earlier, conditional access can require specific authentication strengths rather than just generic MFA.

## Securing registration and SSPR

Strong authentication is great, but you also need to secure the initial registration process. Strong authentication and self-service password reset (SSPR) use slightly different sets of methods, but they've been merged into a single onboarding process — you can get started at `aka.ms/sspr` setup, part of the "My Account" / Security info experience. You can also create registration campaigns to drive adoption of specific authentication methods.

> **Exam tip:** There's a chicken-and-egg problem here: to register for strong authentication, you first have to authenticate — and if that initial authentication uses a weak password, you're using a weak method to onboard into a strong one, which isn't ideal.

One solution: a **temporary access pass (TAP)** — a one-time or limited-use, time-limited code you can generate for a user and share with them offline, which they use to perform that initial authentication and onboard into passwordless technologies. After that, they live entirely in a passwordless world. Under Authentication methods, you can configure the TAP policy — maximum lifetime, whether it's one-time use, and the code length (defaults might be, e.g., an 8-hour maximum lifetime, not one-time use, 8 characters — all configurable). To onboard a new user, you add a new authentication method of type Temporary Access Pass for them, optionally with a delayed start time (e.g., for a Monday-morning start) and a set duration; you then give the code to the user out of band so they can use it, at a designated site, along with their username, to register the Authenticator app or a pass key — after which the pass expires and becomes useless. If they finish early, you can delete it.

You can further lock down where security-information registration itself is allowed to happen, using conditional access user actions targeting "Register security information" — for example, requiring the device to be compliant, or requiring the registration to happen from a certain location (i.e., only on-site) — adding protection to what is a fairly sensitive operation.

If a user forgets their password, and they've already registered for SSPR, there's a self-service site where they can reset it themselves — though the overall direction is toward passwordless rather than relying on passwords at all.

## Conditional Access

Conditional access is arguably the single biggest feature of Entra — it's the policy engine that can apply protection to virtually everything.

> **Exam tip:** Every app you access, every service you access can be evaluated against your defined conditional access policies, and it's triggered for any authorization, regardless of which authentication method was used. It provides very rich controls around users, roles, apps, and environment. This is an Entra ID P1 feature.

Before diving into a policy, it helps to understand the token flow at a high level (you don't need to know it in deep detail). Consider Entra, a user or app (the client), and an app resource the client wants to consume. To use the resource, the client needs a token, which requires authenticating (if not already authenticated) and then being authorized for that particular application.

> **Exam tip:** Conditional access policies are applied every single time a token is requested — everything you do that touches Entra requires conditional access to be evaluated, including any app-specific conditional access policies. Assuming requirements are met, an access token is generated that's time-limited — typically between 60 and 90 minutes, averaging around 75. These tokens are short-lived because, historically, once issued, an access token could not be revoked.

There is a technology called continuous access evaluation that lets supporting apps understand things like location policy changes and sign up for revocation events, so they can cancel the use of a token if something critical happens, by sending a challenge that requires talking back to Entra. Today this is supported mainly by specific (mostly Microsoft) apps, which is why access tokens generally remain very short-lived. A refresh token is used to request a new access token once the current one expires — Entra rechecks conditional access policies, issues a new access token and an updated refresh token, and this rolls forward continuously. If you're using the Microsoft Authentication Library or something like the Authenticator app, all of this token refresh mechanics is handled for you automatically. The bottom line: conditional access is always coming into play, no matter what you're doing.

Within conditional access you also have supporting elements like terms of use (a PDF document you can require users to accept) and named locations (geo-based or IP-based, for restricting access by location). When creating a policy, you give it a name, and assign it to particular users, all users, particular roles, particular groups (with the ability to exclude certain roles), or target only guests. You can target particular cloud applications known to your tenant, target a particular authentication context (which you'd link to particular sensitive data or actions), and it can even link to things like internet sites (covered later). You can configure network requirements, and target based on risk (a P2 feature) — using signals like impossible travel, leaked credentials, or unknown locations. User risk considers online and offline signals; sign-in risk considers whether a particular sign-in looks compromised. You can pull in insider risk information from Purview, target platforms, locations, apps, filter on device attributes (e.g., whether a device is compliant), and hook into authentication flows.

As for controls, you can block access, grant access requiring MFA or a specific authentication strength, require a compliant device, require acceptance of terms of use, require a password change, and more. Session controls let you limit how long a session stays signed in, or customize continuous access evaluation behavior (use these with care).

> **Exam tip:** When you start out with a new conditional access policy, run it in report-only mode first to understand the impact before switching it to enforce (on) — this is the recommended safe rollout approach.

## B2B and External ID

The next area is business-to-business (B2B) collaboration and customers — this all now falls under the "External ID" brand, though B2B and external ID (customer identity) are discussed somewhat separately here for clarity.

Imagine your company wants to collaborate with partners — not customers, but people you want to work with, e.g., on a SharePoint site or on Azure resources. You can invite them into your Entra tenant as a **B2B external identity**. They already have an account within their own company, and they don't want a completely separate account for your company either — that's a bad experience for everyone: another password for them to manage, an account your help desk has to manage and reset passwords for, and their own company loses control over an account being used against your resources.

Instead, they use their existing account. Your tenant has your applications and resources — SharePoint sites, Teams, Azure resources, third-party SaaS apps — trusting it, along with all your own users. Your partner might have their own Entra tenant, a Microsoft consumer account, a Gmail account, a Facebook account, or something using custom SAML/WS-Fed/OpenID Connect — you add them as a B2B external identity into your tenant. You can send them an invitation email they redeem, or set up self-service sign-up based on their organization, letting them sign up automatically for things in your company.

> **Exam tip:** When you do this, the partner's *authentication* happens against their own tenant — they don't need any credentials specific to you — but the *authorization* still happens against your tenant, so your conditional access policies and strong authentication requirements still apply. You can configure trust settings so that, for example, if they've already done strong MFA or are on a compliant device in their own tenant, that signal is sent and made available to you, rather than forcing them through your own MFA again. There's also a one-time passcode option for a partner who has none of these — every time they authenticate, you email them a passcode proving they still control that mailbox; in that specific case, authentication effectively happens against your tenant. This is the exception rather than the norm.

Looking at users in your tenant, external accounts appear with a distinguishing suffix and show their identity source — an email-based one-time passcode identity (e.g., from a Yahoo address), an external Azure AD tenant, Facebook, other external Azure AD sources, a Microsoft account, or phone-based authentication. They appear as objects in your tenant, but the actual account lives elsewhere. Under External identities, you can see and configure the identity providers supported (Entra one-time passcode, Microsoft, Google, Facebook, plus the ability to add custom SAML/WS-Fed providers).

There are general external collaboration settings letting you allow or block certain domains and limit what guests can see, and there's a separate cross-tenant access settings area giving you much finer-grained control over which specific organizations you accept guests from or send guests to, and exactly what you're willing to trust (e.g., whether you trust that MFA was performed, whether you trust compliant devices, whether you trust hybrid-Azure-AD-joined devices). Inbound and outbound access can be overridden per organization on top of your organizational defaults. If a partner tenant is actually owned by your own company, you can even set up cross-tenant sync to automatically populate users as external identities in your tenant, rather than manually adding them.

> **Exam tip:** B2B licensing has changed — it used to depend on a ratio tied to your own internal user license count; now there's a new model: 50,000 free monthly active external users get all the P1/P2 features, and beyond that you're billed based on feature usage (e.g., MFA usage, and the country phone/SMS traffic originates from), tied to a specific subscription for billing.

Then there's the concept of your **customers**. This used to be covered by Azure AD B2C, which has evolved into a more complete customer identity and access management solution, unifying B2C-style capability with the regular Workforce tenant model — customers now live in a dedicated **external tenant**. Licensing is the same as B2B external ID, but you create this separate external tenant type, which supports more customization and more sign-up/sign-in flows. Customers link in their social identities, or create a local account if they don't have one — you don't want customers inside your regular corporate tenant; your customer-facing apps trust this external tenant instead.

Comparing tenant types: a newly created external tenant supports a similar-ish set of identity providers to a Workforce tenant, but adds Apple (common for consumer scenarios, alongside Microsoft, Google, and Facebook). An older-style B2C tenant supports a noticeably wider range of identity providers today, though it's expected that this new external tenant type will gain parity over time.

> **Exam tip:** External tenants are the clear future direction whenever you need identities for customers. B2C still exists, along with its licensing, but you definitely don't want customers in your corporate tenant — you want them in a dedicated external tenant with richer onboarding flows.

## Entitlement management and workflows

**Entitlement management** (a P2 feature) lets you create access packages, bundling things like group memberships, application access, SharePoint sites, and Entra roles. You define policies for who can request them, how they sign up (which can also add them as a guest to your tenant), how many stages of approval are required, and who is allow-listed to get access directly.

**Lifecycle workflows** are useful for automating tasks tied to joiner/mover/leaver scenarios. For example, a "Pre-hire employee" workflow can trigger specific tasks: add a user to a group, disable or enable the account, remove the user, generate a temporary access pass and send it to a private email address (before the employee even starts), send a welcome email, delete the user account, and more — all driven off things like onboarding and offboarding dates that are part of the user's identity data.

> **Exam tip:** Combining entitlement management and lifecycle workflows with dynamic groups and their associated access assignments gives a lot of flexibility for managing the complete lifecycle of a user.

## Internet and Private Access

Internet Access and Private Access are newer features, part of Entra's secure service edge capabilities. A Global Secure Access (GSA) client is installed on client devices and establishes a connection to the Entra edge — with conditional access policies continuing to apply to everything that passes through it.

> **Exam tip:** Because the GSA client subscribes to revocation events, it supports continuous access evaluation in a universal way — access can be terminated in near real time when needed.

**Private Access** targets applications on a private network — on-prem or in a cloud, it doesn't matter. You deploy one or more connectors that establish the connection to the secure service edge, and then make specific resources available — or use "quick access," a broad range of IP addresses and ports, useful, for example, as a first step when replacing a VPN, before narrowing down over time to specific applications actually being used and applying conditional access to just those apps. If you have private DNS, it hooks into your private DNS zones, so name resolution (including single-label names) still works for clients.

> **Exam tip:** The key point of Private Access is completely seamless access to private network resources regardless of where the client is — not an open VPN tunnel with broad blanket access; it's always going through the secure service edge, always evaluated per-request, with conditional access always applied.

**Internet Access** does something similar, but for regular internet traffic. You create security profiles linked to specific sites and categories of websites, tied to policies which are in turn linked via conditional access. Every internet request is checked against whether the site or its category is allowed — including capabilities like TLS inspection down to individual subsites. This protects clients from phishing and token theft, adding a meaningful additional layer of protection for the end user.

## AD in Azure

Even with heavy use of Entra, you probably still need Active Directory in some form. One option is **Entra Domain Services** — used if you have a legacy application and either don't have regular Active Directory Domain Services, or don't want to extend it into your Azure network.

> **Exam tip:** Entra Domain Services is a managed domain — it creates domain controllers, but you don't see them directly. It's a one-way sync from Entra to these domain controllers (no replication back the other way). Services can use it for Kerberos and NTLM. Because it's managed, there are limitations: you can't change the schema, and you don't get domain admin or enterprise admin privileges. You can have multiple replicas — up to five total — across different peered virtual networks (peering is required, forming a mesh replication topology), and they must all be in the same subscription.

The other option is to extend an Active Directory you already have into Azure. There's a basic version: establish connectivity between an Azure virtual network and your on-prem domain controllers (via ExpressRoute private peering or a site-to-site VPN), and point the virtual network's DNS configuration at those domain controllers, giving you both name resolution and domain connectivity. But if you're putting an important service in Azure, you probably don't want a hard dependency on on-prem domain controllers and connectivity.

> **Exam tip:** The more common approach is to add replica domain controllers as regular VMs directly in Azure, point the virtual network's DNS at them, and treat that virtual network as a new Active Directory site (with the connecting link as a new site link) — architecting the correct replication topology so these DCs can talk and replicate objects properly. There's a JSON AD domain-join extension that automatically joins Azure virtual machines to the specified domain and OU, using credentials that should be stored in Key Vault rather than sitting in a plain configuration file — the extension then handles the join automatically.
